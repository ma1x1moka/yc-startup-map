Builds a wearable sensor system that measures actual muscle force output during strength training — not reps counted via accelerometer, not heart rate, but the actual contractile force that determines whether a workout stimulus is effective.

The technical distinction matters: two people performing the same exercise with the same weight can produce dramatically different muscle force outputs depending on tempo, range of motion, and neuromuscular activation. Existing wearables (Apple Watch, Whoop, Garmin) measure external proxies. Fort measures the signal that actually determines training adaptation.

The product reached 1,295 Twitter likes at launch — high for a fitness hardware company, suggesting genuine community interest from strength training enthusiasts who have been frustrated by the shallow data from existing wearables.

Competes for the same serious athlete audience as [Pocket](./Pocket.md) competes for the professional audience — both bet that a purpose-built hardware device extracts more value than a phone app trying to do the same thing.

_Usage:_

"How does Fort differ from existing fitness wearables like Apple Watch or Whoop?"

"Existing wearables like Apple Watch, Whoop, and Garmin measure external proxies like heart rate or rep counts via accelerometers. Fort measures actual contractile muscle force output during strength training. This matters technically because two people lifting the same weight can produce dramatically different muscle force outputs depending on their tempo, range of motion, and neuromuscular activation, so Fort tracks the actual signal determining training adaptation."
